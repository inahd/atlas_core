## What para/diamagnetism actually is

Materials respond to applied magnetic fields in three structurally-distinct ways, governed by the behavior of electrons in their atoms.

**Diamagnetic** materials are very weakly *repelled* by magnetic fields. Every material has some diamagnetic response — it arises from how an applied field perturbs the orbital motion of electrons (Lenz's law operating at the quantum level, with induced currents producing a small field opposing the applied one). When the orbital motion is the only contribution and there are no unpaired electron spins, the material is purely diamagnetic. *Diamagnetic substances:* water, most organic molecules, copper, gold, silver, bismuth, pure carbon (diamond, graphite parallel-plane), most ceramics, most pure silicates without transition-metal impurities. Diamagnetic susceptibilities are small and negative — typically in the range of −10⁻⁵ to −10⁻⁶ in SI units.

**Paramagnetic** materials are weakly *attracted* to magnetic fields. This happens when atoms have unpaired electron spins — those spins act as tiny magnetic dipoles that align (partially, against thermal jostling) with applied fields. Paramagnetism is *additive on top of* the always-present diamagnetic response, and dominates when unpaired spins are present. *Paramagnetic substances:* oxygen (the only common atmospheric gas that's paramagnetic), aluminum, platinum, iron oxides above their magnetic-ordering temperatures, most transition-metal-containing minerals, hemoglobin in its deoxy state, free radicals, most rare-earth-containing materials. Paramagnetic susceptibilities are small and positive — typically 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁵ in SI units, decreasing with temperature according to the Curie law.

**Ferromagnetic** materials are strongly attracted and can become permanently magnetized. They have unpaired spins that *spontaneously align* with each other below the Curie temperature, producing macroscopic magnetic ordering. *Ferromagnetic substances:* iron, nickel, cobalt, magnetite (Fe₃O₄), some rare-earth alloys. Ferromagnetic susceptibilities can be very large — 10² to 10⁵ in SI units — and exhibit hysteresis (the magnetization depends on history, not just present field).

There are also **antiferromagnetic** materials (spins align but in opposing directions, canceling at the macroscale — hematite at room temperature, MnO) and **ferrimagnetic** materials (spins align but with unequal magnitudes producing partial macroscopic moment — magnetite in another regime), but these are subcategories of the ordered-spin family.

The structural hierarchy: *electron spin orientation determines magnetic behavior*. No unpaired spins = diamagnetic. Unpaired spins, no inter-spin coupling = paramagnetic. Unpaired spins with parallel coupling = ferromagnetic. Unpaired spins with antiparallel coupling = antiferromagnetic. *The classifications track increasing degrees of spin-organization in the substrate*.

## The two faces of mineral resonance

Every mineral substrate exhibits resonance properties at *two distinct physical scales* corresponding to two different excitation modes:

**The phonon face** — *acoustic-and-vibrational resonance*. Atoms in the crystal lattice oscillate around their equilibrium positions; these oscillations have specific normal-mode frequencies determined by the lattice symmetry, the atomic masses, and the inter-atomic bonds. These are the modes Raman spectroscopy measures. They sit in the THz range physically (1 cm⁻¹ ≈ 30 GHz, so most Raman modes 100-1500 cm⁻¹ are 3-45 THz). Phonon modes couple to *acoustic and optical excitation* — sound waves, infrared light, mechanical pressure, ultrasound.

**The electromagnetic-coupling face** — *paramagnetic and ferromagnetic resonance*. Unpaired electron spins (when present) precess around the local field at the *Larmor frequency*, which is determined by the spin's effective magnetic moment (the g-factor) and the local field strength. EPR (electron paramagnetic resonance) and FMR (ferromagnetic resonance) measure these. They sit in the GHz range physically for laboratory fields (typical X-band EPR at ~9.4 GHz with applied field of ~3300 Gauss). Magnetic-resonance modes couple to *electromagnetic excitation* — radio waves, microwaves, magnetic fields including ambient geomagnetic and Schumann.

*The two faces are physically distinct but structurally connected*. Both arise from the same crystal lattice with the same atoms in the same positions. The phonon face shows how the *atomic positions* respond to perturbation; the magnetic-resonance face shows how the *electron spins* respond to perturbation. A mineral's complete resonance profile includes both faces.

For the framework, this means: *the harmonic gem analysis we have been doing reads one face. The other face is the one that matters for ambient-field coupling, geomagnetic substrate-resonance, and paramagnetic-soil-effects in agriculture.*

## EPR and FMR — what the data shows for relevant substrates

For paramagnetic-and-ferromagnetic minerals relevant to the framework, the magnetic-resonance behavior has been measured. Key data:

**Iron oxides (the dhātu-bhasma "lauha" family):**
- Magnetite (Fe₃O₄) shows ferromagnetic resonance with **g-factor ≈ 1.86-2.02** depending on particle size and surface conditions. For magnetite nanosuspension, the g-factor was found to be 1.86 ± 0.07. In nanosized iron oxide particles studied by ferromagnetic resonance, three dominant interactions on resonant lines were identified: exchange coupling between Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺ and O⁻ (g₁ = 3.01 ± 0.08), Fe³⁺ centers (g₂ between 1.88 and 2.02 depending on iron oxide states), and flip-flop of O⁻ ions in between ionic states (g₃ between 2.35 and 2.44 depending on iron oxide states).
- Maghemite (γ-Fe₂O₃) shows similar but distinguishable FMR.
- Hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) shows canted antiferromagnetism with characteristic resonance windows distinct from magnetite.

**Translation to physical frequency at terrestrial fields:**
At Earth's geomagnetic field (~50,000 nT = 0.5 Gauss), the Larmor frequency for a free electron (g = 2.00) is about 1.4 MHz. For paramagnetic Fe³⁺ ions in iron-bearing minerals, this translates to *MHz-range coupling at ambient geomagnetic field*. *The resonance windows of paramagnetic minerals overlap with the radio-frequency band of ambient electromagnetic activity*.

## Callahan's paramagnetism in soils — the agricultural foundation

Phil Callahan's work, much of which was published outside mainstream agricultural science, established that *agricultural soils vary widely in paramagnetic content*, measured in CGS units (centimeter-gram-second; specifically the susceptibility ×10⁻⁸).

His core findings:

- **Volcanic-derived soils** (basalt, scoria, certain volcanic ashes) are highly paramagnetic, often >800 CGS units. These soils support unusually robust plant growth.
- **Sedimentary soils** from non-volcanic origins (sandstone-derived, shale-derived) are typically low-paramagnetic, often <200 CGS units.
- **Organic-rich peat and humus** can be either, depending on mineral inclusion.
- **Soils with high-paramagnetic content respond differently to atmospheric electromagnetic activity** — they couple to the ambient field in ways that low-paramagnetic soils do not.

Callahan's central thesis: *paramagnetic soil substrate is what allows plants to couple to atmospheric and cosmic electromagnetic fields*. The plant-as-antenna idea requires a substrate that can transduce ambient EM fields into chemistry the plant uses; paramagnetic minerals provide that transduction. Without paramagnetic soil substrate, the plant's antenna-coupling is degraded, and growth is suboptimal regardless of nutrient availability.
